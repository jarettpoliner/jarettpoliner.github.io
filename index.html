Machine learning for physical systems: reconstructing, representing, and simulating complex physical worlds from sparse observations.

I am a postdoctoral research associate at Los Alamos National Laboratory. I develop machine learning methods for physical systems that can only be partly observed: granular and polymer-bonded materials imaged by micro-CT, plastic solids characterized by a handful of experiments, and urban traffic networks with sparse, unevenly placed sensors. My work combines generative models of 3D microstructure, geometric representations that turn point samples into simulation-ready geometry, and learned models of dynamics that forecast when observations are missing. I care about evaluation as much as modeling: comparing generated structures against measured statistics, and reporting where a method falls short as clearly as where it works.

I received my PhD in Civil Engineering and Engineering Mechanics from Columbia University in 2025, advised by Prof. WaiChing Sun.

Research

A common thread runs through my projects: the physical system is only partially observed, and the useful output is a complete, physically usable state, such as a full 3D microstructure, a closed yield surface, or a forecast at every location in a network. I approach this through generative models that capture what plausible states look like, and through representations (graphs, point clouds, meshes) that keep the result usable by downstream solvers.

01

Generative modeling of material microstructures

Published

Simulating how heterogeneous materials respond to loading requires many realistic 3D microstructures, but micro-CT scans are slow and expensive to obtain. I split microstructure synthesis into two coupled generative problems. An autoregressive graph model, adapted from GraphRNN, generates the grain-contact network. A conditional latent diffusion model then generates each grain's surface as a point cloud, which is meshed into watertight geometry. Using synchrotron micro-CT of a mock polymer-bonded explosive, the paper compares generated and scanned grains on shape, size, and orientation statistics, and generated and measured grain networks on graph-topology statistics.

Paper · Code · Dataset (Zenodo, CC BY 4.0)

02

Geometric reconstruction and super-resolution

Ongoing

Physics solvers need closed, consistent geometry, while scans and generative models usually provide partial or sparse point samples. At Los Alamos I am extending the published pipeline from individual grains to full particle assemblies. One direction uses graph autoencoders that compress a granular packing into a latent representation and reconstruct it. The other is packing super-resolution: a coarse packing is refined by generating smaller particles conditioned on the voids they must fill, under physical constraints such as no interpenetration between contacting grains. Both are ongoing, with manuscripts in preparation.

03

Learning physical dynamics from sparse observations

Ongoing

Real sensor networks are sparse and irregular, and the context that might help a forecast, such as incident reports or traffic-camera images, arrives in very different forms. With Prof. Xuan (Sharon) Di at Columbia, I study traffic forecasting under partial observability. We use a graph-based spatiotemporal model that combines speeds from moving probe vehicles with this auxiliary information. Its evaluation systematically withholds sensors and inputs to measure what each source actually adds, rather than only reporting aggregate accuracy. Experiments cover Manhattan, a Tokyo expressway network with incident labels, and the public METR-LA and PEMS-BAY benchmarks. A manuscript is in preparation.

04

Physics-aware generative modeling

Published

Plasticity models are usually calibrated from only a few expensive experiments or simulations, and fitting a closed-form yield function to sparse data requires repeated trial and error. In our CMAME paper we instead let a latent diffusion model, trained on a library of yield surfaces, infer a dense and plausible surface from the measured yield points. We represent that surface as a mesh rather than an equation. A projection-based stress update designed for mesh-based surfaces then replaces the classical local return-mapping solve. On the paper's benchmarks, which range from classical plasticity models to discrete dislocation dynamics data for single-crystal copper, the update stays stable and runs in parallel. A companion paper (Scientific Reports, in press) turns this into experimental design: Bayesian active learning chooses which simulations to run, and a conditional diffusion model reconstructs the yield surface from the resulting limited data.

Paper (CMAME 2025) · Scientific Reports paper in press
